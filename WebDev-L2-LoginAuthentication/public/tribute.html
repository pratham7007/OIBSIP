Katherine Johnson

A mathematician whose careful calculations helped make early human spaceflight possible.

Her story

Mathematics in motion

Curiosity, early and often

Katherine Johnson showed an exceptional ability with numbers from a young age. Because her hometown did not offer high school for Black students, her family moved so she and her siblings could continue their education.

A mathematician at Langley

In 1953, Johnson joined the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics, later part of NASA. She worked in a group of Black women mathematicians whose calculations supported the agency’s aeronautics and space programs.

Precision with purpose

Her work included checking flight paths and trajectories. When astronaut John Glenn prepared for his 1962 orbital mission, he asked for Johnson to verify the computer-generated numbers by hand—a powerful sign of the trust her expertise had earned.

A legacy that reaches forward

Johnson continued to contribute to NASA programs through the Apollo era and the Space Shuttle program. Her career widened recognition of the people and mathematical work behind space exploration.

Milestones

A life of discovery

1918
Born in White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia

Johnson’s early love of mathematics would shape her education and career.

1937
Graduated from West Virginia State College

She completed degrees in mathematics and French at age 18.

1953
Joined the Langley research center

Her calculations supported the growing U.S. aerospace research effort.

1962
Verified John Glenn’s orbital trajectory

Her hand-checked calculations helped confirm the flight plan for Friendship 7.

2015
Received the Presidential Medal of Freedom

The honor recognized her contributions to the nation and to science.
